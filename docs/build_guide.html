OpenVision · hardware build guide · version 2026-11-04

Build the OpenVision glasses camera

A Wi-Fi camera with a push button that clips onto ordinary glasses. It sends what you see to the AI model running in Chrome on your Galaxy S24 FE. Tap the button and the phone describes the scene out loud. Hold it and you can ask a question.

≈ 1.1–1.5 M Tparts cost (Nov 2026, Iran)
4–6 hwork, plus print time
≈ 18 gon one arm of the glasses
66×31×13 mmcamera box · fits any glasses

How it works in one paragraph

The ESP32-CAM board (a small Wi-Fi chip plus a 2-megapixel camera) sits in a 3D-printed box on the outside of the temple arm, just behind the hinge. It makes its own Wi-Fi network, OpenVision-Cam. The phone joins that network, and the OpenVision page in Chrome fetches a picture whenever you press the button. The board gets power from the phone through a thin USB cable that runs along the arm and behind your ear. Everything stays offline on the phone.

No calipers needed. The box is sized from the official Ai-Thinker datasheet (board 27 × 40.5 × 4.5 mm ±0.2), with room to spare. It's held on by two straps that wrap around the box and the temple arm, so it fits almost any glasses (arms about 1.5–8 mm thick and 2–15 mm tall, metal or plastic). A ruler with mm marks is enough for the few checks.

3 · Program the board and test it on the desk

Do this first, while the board still has its pins. If it doesn't work on the desk, it won't work on the glasses.

3.1Install ESP32 support in Arduino IDE

  1. Open Arduino IDE → File → Preferences.
  2. In Additional boards manager URLs, paste:
    https://espressif.github.io/arduino-esp32/package_esp32_index.json
  3. Tools → Board → Boards Manager, search esp32, and install "esp32 by Espressif Systems" (the newest version).

3.2Open and edit the sketch

  1. File → Open → firmware/openvision_cam/openvision_cam.ino.
  2. Find AP_PASS = "openvision123" and change it to your own password, at least 8 characters. Write it down; you'll need it on the phone and for later updates.
  3. Leave everything else as it is for now.

3.3Connect and choose the exact settings

  1. Fit the ESP32-CAM into the MB board (camera facing up, pins lined up), then plug the micro-USB cable into the MB and the PC.
  2. Set the Tools menu exactly like this:
Tools menu itemSet to
Boardesp32 → ESP32 Wrover Module
PSRAMEnabled
Partition SchemeMinimal SPIFFS (1.9MB APP with OTA / 190KB SPIFFS)
Upload Speed460800 (use 115200 if uploads fail)
Portthe new COM port that appears when you plug in the MB
  1. Click Upload (→). If it stops at Connecting… or says Failed to connect: hold the IO0 button on the MB, click Upload again, and let go when the percentage starts counting.
  2. When you see Hard resetting via RTS pin…, press the RST button on the ESP32-CAM.
  3. Open Tools → Serial Monitor at 115200 baud. You should see:
    Wi-Fi "OpenVision-Cam" – camera at http://192.168.4.1
    If you see Camera init failed instead, the camera ribbon isn't fully in. Flip up the black latch, push the ribbon in (contacts facing the board), close the latch, and press RST.

3.4Desk test with the phone

  1. Remove the clear protective film from the camera lens (there's often a small tab on it).
  2. On the phone: Settings → Connections → Wi-Fi → OpenVision-Cam → enter your password. If Android says "no internet", choose Keep connection / Stay connected.
  3. Open Chrome and go to http://192.168.4.1. You should see "OpenVision camera is running" and a picture.
  4. If the page won't load: turn off mobile data for a moment and try again (see Troubleshooting).
  5. If the picture is upside down, set FLIP_V = true and MIRROR_H = true in the sketch and upload again. It's easier to fix now, while the pins are still on.

3.5Test the button on the desk

  1. Take a push button and touch two of its diagonally opposite legs to the IO13 and GND pins (wires or jumper leads), with the Serial Monitor open.
  2. A short press should print button: down then button: tap. A press of half a second or more should print button: down, button: long, button: up.
Use IO13, never IO12. IO12 is a boot pin, and if it's pulled the wrong way the board won't start. The pins are labelled on the back of the board.

4.2Set the model up in OpenSCAD

  1. Open hardware/glasses_cam_mount.scad in OpenSCAD, then Window → Customizer (make sure it's ticked).
  2. Check these settings (they're already the defaults):
    • cam_type = esp32cam · mount_style = strap · preset = custom
    • side = right (or left, for the arm you'll wear it on)
    • tilt = 10 (the camera points 10° down, which suits walking and reading)
  3. Keep part = preview and press F5. The see-through bar is a temple arm, and the two see-through rings are the straps going around the box and the arm.

4.5Test-fit the print before any electronics go in

  • The lid's rim drops into the box opening.
  • A zip-tie (or O-ring) fits between each pair of fins on the top and bottom of the box.
  • Hold the empty box against the outside of the arm, just behind the hinge, and wrap the two straps around box + arm (section 6.4). It shouldn't twist when you push the front.

5 · Wiring and soldering

5.1Cut the header pins

  1. Only do this after the desk test passed (section 3). From now on, updates go over Wi-Fi (section 9).
  2. Take the board off the MB. With flush cutters, cut every pin as short as you can on both rows. Leave about 1 mm on 5V, GND and IO13 so you have something to solder to.
  3. Brush away the metal bits. Hold the board up to a light and check that no two pins are bridged.
  4. With a ruler, check the board is no thicker than 6.5 mm now (the box is made for that). If it's thicker, file the pin stubs down.

5.2Prepare and check the cable

  1. Cut the cable so that, with the USB-A plug at one end, it's long enough to run from the hinge, along the arm, behind your ear and down to your pocket (usually 1.2 m).
  2. Strip 25 mm of the outer jacket. Inside you'll find red, black, green and white wires (sometimes a foil shield too, which you cut off).
  3. Check the colours with the multimeter: plug the USB-A end into a phone charger and measure between red and black. You should read +5.0 V (±0.25), with red on the multimeter's red probe. Some cheap cables use odd colours, so trust the meter, not the colour.
  4. Cut green and white back to 5 mm and cover them with heat-shrink.
  5. Thread the cable in through the exit slot at the back-bottom of the box before you solder anything. Then pull a 2.5 mm zip-tie tight around the cable about 8 mm from the end of the jacket and trim its tail. That's the strain relief: the zip-tie can't pass back through the slot.

5.3Solder (power off)

  1. Capacitor: trim its legs to 6 mm. Solder red to the long (+) leg and black to the striped (−) leg. Put heat-shrink over each joint.
  2. From the capacitor legs, run two 28 AWG wires about 35 mm long: + → 5V pin, − → GND pin.
  3. Button: bend all 4 legs out flat. Solder two 28 AWG wires about 45 mm long to two diagonally opposite legs. With the multimeter on continuity, check that it beeps only while you press.
  4. One button wire goes to IO13. Join the other to the GND wire or GND pin (there's a second GND pin on the other row you can use too).
  5. Power test before closing: plug the USB-A end into a charger. Within about 5 s the phone should see the OpenVision-Cam Wi-Fi network again.
Never connect the 5 V wire to the 3V3 pin, and never reverse red and black. Either one can kill the board instantly.

6 · Assemble the box and fit it to the glasses

6.1Board

  1. Put a strip of 1 mm foam tape on the inner wall of the board area.
  2. Lay the board in with the antenna end toward the back (the ear) and the camera end toward the front, metal shield facing out toward the lid.

6.2Button and capacitor

  1. Sit the button on the block in the back chamber, plunger facing out toward the lid. Put the lid on loosely to check that the plunger pokes through its hole by about 1 mm. Then fix the button to the block with a dot of hot glue on its sides (not on the plunger).
  2. Lay the capacitor in the channel below the block, with the cable and zip-tie behind it.

6.3Camera

  1. Gently bend the camera ribbon 90° forward. Make it a smooth curve, never a sharp crease, and never bend right next to the connector.
  2. Seat the camera module flat against the angled front face, with the lens in the hole. Before gluing, plug in power, open http://192.168.4.1 and check the picture is upright and centred.
  3. Fix it with two small dots of CA glue on the edges of the camera PCB. Keep glue well away from the lens.
  4. If the ribbon won't reach without straining, use the 75 mm-ribbon OV2640 (shopping list #11). It plugs into the same connector.

6.4Close and strap it to the glasses

  1. Press the lid on (rim inside). No glue needed, because the straps hold it.
  2. Stick the anti-slip pad (foam or inner-tube rubber) onto the flat inner face of the box, the side with no opening.
  3. Hold the box against the outside of the temple arm, front edge about 5 mm behind the hinge, lens pointing forward. Line the arm up with the lens hole height: the lens should sit level with your eye or slightly below.
  4. Thread a zip-tie between the front pair of fins: over the top of the box, down the inner side of the arm, under the bottom, and back up over the lid. Put the zip-tie head on the lid side, where it can't touch your head. Pull it snug but don't crush the arm. Do the same at the rear fins.
  5. Check: the box doesn't twist when you push it, and the glasses still fold. Trim the zip-tie tails flush with no sharp edge. (With O-rings: just stretch them over box + arm in the same two places.)
  6. Run the cable back along the arm and hold it with 3–4 small zip-ties or O-rings: mid-arm, just before the ear, and 1–2 on the curve behind the ear. Leave a small loop at the hinge so the arm can still fold.
  7. Down to the phone: USB-A plug → OTG adapter → phone. The phone stays in your pocket.

7 · Set up the phone (Galaxy S24 FE)

The page has to be opened from http://localhost. Chrome blocks an https:// website from talking to the camera's plain http:// address. Termux gives the phone its own small local web server.

7.1Copy the app to the phone

  1. Connect the phone to the PC with USB, create the folder Download/OpenVision on the phone, and copy index.html into it.

7.2Termux (one time)

  1. Install F-Droid from f-droid.org, then install Termux from F-Droid.
  2. Open Termux and type these lines, pressing Enter after each: 
    pkg update -y
pkg install -y python
termux-setup-storage
    Tap Allow when it asks for file access.

7.3Start the app (every time)

  1. In Termux:
    cd ~/storage/downloads/OpenVision
python -m http.server 8080
    Pull down the Termux notification and tap Acquire wakelock, so Android doesn't stop it.
  2. In Chrome open http://localhost:8080/index.html. Use ⋮ → Add to Home screen so it's one tap next time.

7.4Download the AI model (one time, on normal internet)

  1. While the phone is on your normal Wi-Fi (not the camera's), choose Qwen3-VL 2B and tap Load. The download is about 1–2 GB and Chrome keeps it afterwards.
  2. Wait for "Loaded… Runs fully on this phone now".

7.5Voice (one time)

  1. Persian voice output: Settings → General management → Text-to-speech → Preferred engine: Speech Services by Google → ⚙ → Install voice data → Persian. If Persian isn't offered, install the sherpa-onnx TTS Engine app with a Persian Piper voice and make it the preferred engine.
  2. Offline speech input: Google app → Settings → Voice → Offline speech recognition → download Persian.

7.6Connect to the glasses

  1. Plug the glasses cable into the phone (OTG). Wait about 5 s.
  2. Wi-Fi → OpenVision-Cam → Keep connection when Android warns about no internet.
  3. In the app: Settings → Camera → "ESP32 Wi-Fi camera (glasses)", with the address 192.168.4.1.
  4. The pill next to the address should turn green: "connected · button on".

8 · First use and final checks

You doWhat should happen
Tap the glasses buttonA thumbnail appears, the screen shows "Thinking…", then the phone describes the scene in 1–2 sentences and reads it aloud.
Hold it (≥ 0.5 s)The picture from the moment you pressed is kept, the microphone opens ("Listening…"), and you ask your question out loud.
Press again while listeningThe phone stops listening and answers.
Press while it is talkingThe speech stops and a new picture is taken.

Final checklist

  • The pill shows "connected · button on".
  • The picture is upright, sharp, and aimed where you look (if it's too high or low, change tilt and reprint).
  • 10 presses in a row: every tap is described, every hold asks.
  • The arm still folds, and the cable doesn't pull when you turn your head.
  • After 20 minutes the box is warm but comfortable on your temple.
  • Offline test: airplane mode on, then Wi-Fi back on to OpenVision-Cam only. Reload the page; it should still load and describe. If it doesn't, keep mobile data on (see Troubleshooting).

9 · Updating the firmware over Wi-Fi (pins are cut)

  1. Power the glasses camera (phone or any USB charger).
  2. Connect the PC to the Wi-Fi OpenVision-Cam.
  3. In Arduino IDE: Tools → Port → under Network ports, choose openvision-cam at 192.168.4.1. Keep the same board settings as in 3.3.
  4. Click Upload. When asked for a password, enter your AP_PASS. The board restarts by itself.
  5. If the network port doesn't show up: restart Arduino IDE, and allow it through the Windows Firewall when asked.
If you change AP_PASS, the next over-the-air update needs the old password, and the phone will need the new one.
